# Before Earth

By the time the fleets gathered for the final push to Earth, the galaxy had learned a hard truth: a Reaper could die.

That knowledge had not come easily. It had been purchased in fire, in cities turned to ash, in broken hulls drifting soundless through vacuum. It had taken years for the lesson to sink in, because the first proof had seemed like a miracle, and miracles were difficult things to build a war on.

The first had been Sovereign.

Even now, in the war room of the *Normandy*, veterans still spoke the name with a strange mixture of hatred and gratitude. Hatred, because Sovereign had opened the door. Gratitude, because it had bled.

Commander Shepard remembered the Citadel as flashes of light and fragments of impact alarms, remembered the impossible silhouette of the Reaper hanging over the station like a god lowering its head to judge the living. Sovereign had spoken with the certainty of extinction. It had called itself beyond death, beyond comprehension, beyond fear.

And then the fleets had torn into it.

Not quickly. Not cleanly. Every ship that had fired on Sovereign had done so under the understanding that they might be dead in the next second. The Destiny Ascension had burned. Alliance ships had broken apart like toys under a boot. The Citadel itself had become a battlefield, steel and vacuum and bodies tumbling in artificial night. But when the last of Sovereign's defenses failed and its carcass hit the station, the galaxy had seen something it was never meant to see.

A dead Reaper.

At the time, it had felt singular, almost mythical, as if history would never allow such a moment twice.

History, Shepard had learned, could be persuaded.

The second kill had belonged to the asari.

It happened months into the Reaper War, after Thessia had already begun to drown under red skies and orbital fire. The asari navy had been bloodied, split between evacuation corridors, defensive screens, and desperate delaying actions around colony worlds nobody in the Hierarchy or the Alliance could even spare a frigate for anymore. But asari commanders had always understood patience. They knew how to disappear when brute strength failed. They knew how to wait until pride became a weapon.

The Reaper they trapped had descended on a relay approach near a veil of fractured debris left behind by an earlier battle. It was hunting for survivors, for wounded ships, for anything foolish enough to signal. Instead, it found silence.

Then the silence opened fire.

Four asari dreadnoughts had hidden cold among the wreckage with their drives dark and their crews strapped in for nearly eleven hours, waiting for the Reaper to move inside the kill box their admirals had sketched on a tactical display and then, with no better word for it, prayed over.

The ambush began with two firing on its forward arc, forcing the Reaper to angle toward them. That exposed its flank to the other pair.

For seventeen seconds, the void became white.

Thanix impacts stripped armor in molten sheets. Kinetic barriers flared and collapsed. One dreadnought, the *Athame's Spear*, took a return beam through the spine and broke apart so quickly its distress burst never fully transmitted. Another lost engines and spun helplessly through the debris field, still firing broadside as it tumbled. The asari crews did not break formation. They closed the trap instead, hammering the same wounded section again and again with the cold concentration of surgeons cutting around a tumor.

When the Reaper finally tried to climb out of the pocket, it was too late. A coordinated salvo from all four dreadnoughts punched through the gap they had made in its armor, deep enough to reach something vital and ancient inside.

The Reaper came apart in shards of burning metal and violet static.

The surviving asari ships were too damaged to cheer. They had rescue boats in the water within minutes, pulling crew from ruptured compartments and drifting escape pods. But the news spread faster than the medevac reports. On refugee ships. On fleet channels. In trench lines. In hospital wards. Another dead Reaper.

Not a miracle this time.

A method.

The third kill belonged to the turians, and if the asari death had been elegant, the turian one was savage.

It happened on Palaven, under a sky so thick with smoke and cinders that sunrise looked like an open wound. The Reaper had landed in the ruins beyond a shattered anti-air complex, using the broken highlands as a staging ground to direct husk swarms toward the evacuation routes. Turian air support couldn't get close. Their artillery had already been pounded flat. Every clean solution had burned away in the first weeks.

So the turians used what remained.

The weapon began as a joke, according to the troopers who survived long enough to retell it. Somebody in the 9th Ground Support Legion had looked at a crippled mass accelerator emplacement, three downed cargo lifters, a warehouse of unstable eezo-bored mining charges, and a toppled comms tower, and said that if the Reaper wanted Palaven, they ought to drop part of Palaven on its head.

Under any sane conditions, the plan would have been dismissed.

Palaven had not seen sane conditions in a long time.

Engineers, infantry, and two blackened-faced mechanics from a unit that no longer existed worked for nine straight hours under intermittent bombardment. They rewired the mass accelerator's crippled firing assembly, stripped the lifters for intact field generators, and used the comms tower as a crude guidance spine. The mining charges were packed into the tower's hollow frame until every handler involved was certain the whole thing would blow before it ever launched.

General Corinthus, who would later deny he approved the operation, gave it his blessing in person.

When the Reaper advanced on the ridge at dusk, the turians let it come.

Ground teams lured it forward with anything that could still shoot: shoulder batteries, sniper teams, even a pair of armored vehicles running on patched coolant lines and bad luck. The Reaper answered with contempt. It strode over burning earth and shattered gun nests, each step shaking dust from half-collapsed bunkers. Its beam carved trenches of glass across the hillside. Turian squads died buying seconds.

Then the improvised weapon fired.

The old mass accelerator screamed like it was tearing the planet open. The comms tower, wrapped in eezo charges and held together by desperation, punched downrange on a halo of unstable blue light. It should not have flown as straight as it did. It should not have survived long enough to hit.

But war was full of impossible things.

The tower drove into the Reaper low in the chest, not enough to kill it outright, but deep enough to anchor the charges inside its armor. A half-second later, every mining charge cooked off at once.

Witnesses would argue forever about what happened next. Some said the blast lifted the Reaper off the ground. Others said its scream came over every open comm channel at once, a sound of metal grieving. What everyone agreed on was the sight: the machine staggering, one leg failing, armor peeled outward like a flower of black iron. Before it could recover, turian spotters redirected the last functioning guns in the sector. Artillery, anti-air batteries, portable missile teams, and infantry accelerators all struck the wound together.

The Reaper toppled onto the plains of Palaven and did not rise again.

By the time the allied fleets assembled over Earth, those three victories had become more than stories. They were doctrine. Proof stacked on proof. Sovereign had taught the galaxy that Reapers were not gods. The asari had taught it that precision and discipline could break one in open space. The turians had taught it that even on a dying world, with scrap and nerve and half a plan, ground forces could still drag one down.

That was why the mood before the final battle was not hope exactly, 

Belief.

In the strategy rooms and troop bays, among quarians, krogan, turians, humans, salarians, and asari, the old fear had changed shape. The Reapers were still terrible. They were still older than memory and stronger than anything the galaxy had built.

But they were not immortal.

The soldiers boarding shuttles for London carried that truth with them like a weapon of its own. Somewhere behind every prayer and every rifle check was the image of Sovereign's broken shell on the Citadel, the burning ruin of the asari ambush drifting through the stars, and the corpse of the Palaven Reaper sprawled across the blasted earth.

Three dead giants.

Enough to remind the living that the end of the story had not been written by the enemy yet.
